Executive Brief: The 4-Move Childcare Cascade

Core thesis: childcare can be stabilized fastest when the White House acts as a friction-removal engine instead of a centralized operator.

1. Declare childcare as workforce infrastructure

Shift the frame from welfare debate to labor supply, family formation, productivity, military readiness, and local resilience.

2. Create a federal template stack

One-page state waivers, model zoning language, insurance pool design, employer tax pathways, provider startup kit.

3. Trigger pilots through incentives

Reward states, cities, employers, and civic buildings that create safe verified slots quickly.

4. Publish a scoreboard

Slots opened, waitlists reduced, family cost burden reduced, provider closures avoided, caregiver wages improved.

What “minimal effort” looks like at the presidential level

The president does not need to design every program. The minimum viable sequence is: issue a public priority signal, order interagency coordination, require agencies to identify existing authorities, convene governors and employers, direct procurement preference exploration, and request Congress package childcare incentives into must-pass economic legislation.

What to avoid

Avoid a single giant program that becomes an ideological target. Avoid subsidies detached from supply creation. Avoid “deregulation” language that appears to reduce child safety. Avoid federal micromanagement that lets states resist. Avoid pilot programs with no public measurement layer.

Metasystems Questions for Maximum Cascadability

These are the highest-leverage questions to ask because they find the smallest action that unlocks the largest downstream system response.

1. Question Spine

What already exists, who can authorize it, what blocks it, what incentive makes it self-propagating, and how do we measure dignity-preserving success?

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make question spine easier for states to implement?
  • Has every proposal in this document actually been checked against all five parts of this spine, or just the easy parts?
  • Where the spine can't be answered yet for a given idea, is that gap flagged honestly instead of skipped over?
  • Who reviews whether “dignity-preserving success” was actually measured, rather than assumed?
2. Minimal Lift Doctrine

Use directives, templates, incentives, procurement, liability pooling, and public scoreboards before building new bureaucracy.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make minimal lift doctrine easier for states to implement?
  • What is the smallest existing tool — directive, waiver, template — that hasn't been tried yet for this specific bottleneck?
  • What would have to be true for a brand-new program to actually be justified here, instead of a lighter-touch option?
  • Who is accountable if “minimal lift” quietly turns into no lift at all?
3. Supply First

Childcare affordability cannot be solved only by demand subsidies when the provider base is too thin.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make supply first easier for states to implement?
  • Is a proposed subsidy actually paired with a supply-side action, or does it stand alone?
  • What current wait time or vacancy rate would prove new supply is genuinely being created, not just funded?
  • Which existing providers could expand fastest with the least new investment?
4. Workforce Ladder

Fast-track safe caregivers through tiered credentials, wage ladders, apprenticeships, substitute pools, and retirement-to-care pathways.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make workforce ladder easier for states to implement?
  • What specific prior experience — military, retail, healthcare aide — should count toward credential hours here, and has anyone mapped it?
  • Does the fast-track shorten paperwork, or does it quietly shorten actual training?
  • Who tracks caregiver retention after the fast-track, not just initial hiring numbers?
5. Insurance Unlock

Treat liability coverage as critical infrastructure; pool risk and standardize safety verification.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make insurance unlock easier for states to implement?
  • What is the current cost or availability barrier facing a small provider trying to get insured today?
  • Who bears the risk if a shared pool's claims run higher than projected?
  • What standard safety verification would insurers actually accept in place of case-by-case underwriting?
6. Zoning Unlock

Auto-approve small childcare nodes in safe residential, faith, school, and mixed-use spaces unless a concrete safety problem exists.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make zoning unlock easier for states to implement?
  • What specific objection categories are legitimate grounds to block a small node, and which aren't?
  • How quickly must an objection be raised before auto-approval proceeds?
  • Who monitors whether “unless objected to” is quietly being used to exclude certain neighborhoods?
7. Employer Cascade

Make childcare an economic productivity tool through tax and procurement advantages.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make employer cascade easier for states to implement?
  • What specific tax or procurement lever is actually available today without new legislation?
  • Is participation measured by dollars committed, or by slots actually created?
  • Does the incentive favor large employers by default, and if so, what's the plan for small businesses?
8. Federalism Bridge

Let states administer, but make the federal template frictionless and politically attractive.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make federalism bridge easier for states to implement?
  • What is the one federal template a state would actually adopt as-is, versus rewrite from scratch?
  • Who resolves it when a state's existing law conflicts with the federal template?
  • How is success measured fairly across states with very different starting rules?
9. Family Choice

Support center care, home care, grandparent care, stay-at-home care, co-ops, and flexible work without forcing one model.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make family choice easier for states to implement?
  • Does current subsidy design actually treat home care, co-ops, and center care equally, or favor one by default?
  • What specific rule currently penalizes a family for choosing a non-standard care model?
  • Who represents family preference in decisions that are otherwise made by providers or agencies alone?
10. Measurement Layer

Track parent work stability, provider slots, waitlists, price burden, child safety, caregiver pay, and commute distance.

  • What is the smallest federal signal that would make measurement layer easier for states to implement?
  • Which of these metrics is currently being tracked at all today, versus simply assumed?
  • Who audits the dashboard numbers independently, rather than the agency reporting on its own progress?
  • What happens, concretely, when a metric shows a program isn't working?

Step-by-Step Playbooks by Power Layer

President / White House

Use speech, executive order, cabinet tasking, governor convening, employer roundtable, and budget signal.

  1. Issue a presidential memorandum directing HHS, GSA, and DOD to inventory underused federal real estate suitable for licensed care within 30 days.
  2. Convene a one-time summit of governors, mayors, and major employers to publicly announce matching pilot commitments.
  3. Direct OMB to identify existing appropriations and waiver authorities that can fund supply-side pilots without new legislation.
  4. Use the annual budget request to propose a supply-side childcare fund alongside existing demand-side subsidies.
  5. Frame the effort in a major address around workforce participation and family stability to build durable, cross-party support.
  6. Ask the Domestic Policy Council to publish a public 90-day scoreboard of federal actions taken and slots created.
  7. Direct large federal employers (VA, DOD, Postal Service) to pilot on-site or after-hours childcare on their own properties first.
  8. Instruct agencies to publish reusable model legal, insurance, and inspection templates so states and cities aren't reinventing them.
Chief of Staff

Reduce the ask to one dashboard, one memo, one agency owner, one weekly cadence.

  1. Assign single-owner accountability inside the West Wing so the initiative can't stall from diffused responsibility.
  2. Put childcare capacity on a standing weekly senior-staff agenda until the first pilots actually launch.
  3. Broker disputes between agencies (HHS vs. GSA vs. DOD) quickly, rather than letting them stall in email chains.
  4. Protect the initiative's visibility with a recurring Presidential briefing, not a one-time announcement that fades.
  5. Set a hard internal deadline for the first public dashboard, and personally enforce it.
  6. Pre-clear rollout messaging with political and legislative affairs so it doesn't stall on wording disputes.
  7. Track which agencies are behind schedule and escalate blockers directly instead of waiting for status reports.
OMB

Map existing funds and rule flexibilities; prevent new programs from duplicating old bottlenecks.

  1. Map every existing appropriation and waiver authority that already touches childcare supply, not just subsidies.
  2. Flag where a proposed new pilot would duplicate an existing but underused program instead of funding something genuinely new.
  3. Fast-track OIRA review for any rule change that unlocks supply (e.g., federal space licensing) instead of letting it queue normally.
  4. Require agencies proposing new childcare spending to show how it creates new slots, not just subsidizes already-scarce ones.
  5. Build one shared cost-per-new-slot scoring method so competing agency proposals can actually be compared.
  6. Identify apportionment flexibility that lets agencies redirect underused facilities funding toward conversion costs.
Congressional Leadership

Bundle childcare supply, insurance, and employer tax incentives into economic competitiveness packages.

  1. Bundle childcare supply provisions into a bipartisan economic-competitiveness or workforce bill rather than a standalone childcare bill.
  2. Use committee hearings to spotlight working state and local pilots as proof of concept before asking for federal dollars.
  3. Attach employer tax incentives for shared childcare investment to existing small-business or tax-extender legislation.
  4. Direct GAO to audit which existing federal programs already fund supply-side childcare, so new legislation doesn't duplicate them.
  5. Keep any pilot appropriation small, specific, and time-limited to protect it from becoming an earmark fight.
  6. Recruit co-sponsors from both parties' state delegations by framing this as local jobs and family stability, not a partisan program.
Governors

Adopt model zoning/licensing reciprocity and launch slot-creation scoreboards.

  1. Issue an executive order directing the state licensing agency to publish a fast-track pathway for converting compliant buildings.
  2. Adopt licensing reciprocity so a caregiver credentialed in one county or state doesn't have to re-certify to work in another.
  3. Launch a public statewide scoreboard tracking new slots by county to create friendly competition among mayors.
  4. Direct state-owned sites (DMV offices, community colleges, park visitor centers) to host after-hours pilots first.
  5. Where legally available, use emergency-style procurement authority to compress permitting timelines for the first pilot cohort.
  6. Convene the state's largest employers to co-invest in a shared regional childcare node near a major job center.
Mayors/County Executives

Open public building inventories and fast-track small provider permits.

  1. Publish a public inventory of underused city-owned buildings — libraries, rec centers, closed schools — available for care use.
  2. Create a single fast-track permit desk so a small provider isn't routed through five separate departments.
  3. Waive or reduce permitting fees for the first cohort of pilot providers to lower launch friction.
  4. Convene local employers and faith institutions to co-locate a shared node near a major job or transit hub.
  5. Set a public, dated target for new licensed slots and report progress at a recurring public meeting.
  6. Partner with the local workforce board to fast-track caregiver training and job placement into new slots.
Employers

Treat childcare as labor retention infrastructure; co-fund shared nodes near shift clusters.

  1. Co-fund a shared childcare node with nearby employers instead of building a single-company center alone.
  2. Offer a childcare stipend or backup-care benefit as a near-term bridge while a permanent node is built.
  3. Align care hours with actual shift schedules, including nights and weekends, not just standard business hours.
  4. Partner with an existing licensed provider rather than becoming one, to avoid taking on licensing liability directly.
  5. Publish participation and retention/attendance data so other employers can see the business case for joining.
  6. Advocate with local government for the specific zoning and permitting changes that make employer-adjacent care possible.
Providers

Use shared services, apprenticeships, micro-center models, and transparent safety reporting.

  1. Join or form a shared-services cooperative to split payroll, compliance, and purchasing costs with nearby providers.
  2. Use an apprenticeship or tiered-credential path to bring in new caregivers faster without lowering safety standards.
  3. Adopt a transparent parent-facing safety and incident-reporting practice ahead of any regulatory requirement to do so.
  4. Document the specific rule or inspection step that is the actual bottleneck to opening new slots, and report it upward.
  5. Explore micro-center or mixed-age models where licensing allows, to use existing space more efficiently.
  6. Participate in pilot data collection — real provider-level evidence is what ultimately justifies scaling the policy.

R&D Bridge: From Research to Operating Rules

This bridge turns high-level R&D into implementable design rules.

Behavioral economics

Make the easy option the good option: default-approved templates, prefilled applications, automatic eligibility prompts, social proof scoreboards.

Implementation question: Which specific form or approval step could become the new default within 30 days, without waiting for new software?

Operations research

Optimize location, staffing, opening hours, and transport against parent commute patterns and workforce shifts.

Implementation question: Which one scheduling or staffing constraint, if optimized first, would unlock the most capacity fastest?

Public finance

Pair subsidies with supply-side expansion so scarce slots do not simply absorb new money through price pressure.

Implementation question: Which existing subsidy dollar could be redirected toward creating a new slot instead of bidding on an existing one?

Insurance systems

Pool liability risk, standardize incident data, and reward verified safety practices with lower premiums.

Implementation question: Which single provider or cohort could pilot pooled liability coverage first, to start building real claims data?

Labor economics

Treat caregiver wages and training as capacity constraints; low pay produces turnover, turnover destroys quality and supply.

Implementation question: Which specific wage or training barrier is most responsible for current caregiver turnover in this system?

Developmental science

Preserve child safety, stable relationships, language-rich environments, nutrition, sleep, and predictable routines.

Implementation question: Which safety or stability standard must never be shortened, no matter how much administrative friction it removes?

Urban planning

Put childcare where families already live, work, commute, worship, learn, and receive services.

Implementation question: Which single existing building, already near where families live and work, is closest to being usable today?

Complex systems

Use many safe micro-nodes rather than depending exclusively on large centers that require high capital and long timelines.

Implementation question: Which one small, safe pilot node could prove the model before any large, capital-intensive center is proposed?

Data governance

Track only necessary metrics, protect family privacy, and prevent punitive surveillance of parents/providers.

Implementation question: Which specific data field currently collected serves no safety or eligibility purpose and could be removed first?

AI / automation

Use AI for paperwork simplification, slot matching, inspection scheduling, translation, eligibility triage, and fraud detection with human appeal.

Implementation question: Which single repetitive task — not a judgment call — is the clearest first candidate for automation with human review?

Pilot Library: 12 Replicable Childcare Unlocks

Each pilot is designed to be politically legible, locally adaptable, and measurable within one quarter.

Federal Building After-Hours Childcare Conversion

Purpose

Convert federal office space that already sits empty evenings and weekends into licensed care capacity, without waiting on new construction.

Setup

  1. GSA identifies three to five federal buildings with existing fire-code and security compliance.
  2. The state licensing agency pre-approves a modular after-hours-use permit specific to federal sites.
  3. The first users are agency employees, with nearby employers invited once the first cohort is running.
  4. A single point of contact coordinates between the building's facilities office and the childcare operator.

Safety Protocol

A secure after-hours entrance separate from general building access, sign-in/sign-out matched to the building's own security log, a fire marshal sign-off specific to after-hours occupancy, and the same caregiver ratios as a standalone center.

Cascade Trigger

Once one agency's building runs cleanly for a full quarter, GSA publishes the after-hours permit template so any federal facility nationwide can adopt it without renegotiating from scratch.

Employer Shift-Matched Childcare Compact

Purpose

Solve the specific mismatch between standard 9-to-5 center hours and the night, rotating, and weekend shifts that leave many shift workers without any care option at all.

Setup

  1. Three to five employers with overlapping shift patterns (a hospital, a distribution center, a call center) commit jointly.
  2. Map the combined shift calendar first, and size the node to that calendar rather than a standard school-day schedule.
  3. Co-locate the node near the shared shift-change point (a transit hub or shared campus) to minimize detour time.
  4. Set a joint funding formula proportional to each employer's shift-worker headcount.

Safety Protocol

Overnight and early-morning staffing ratios verified independently of daytime ratios, since fatigue and lower staffing density are the specific added risk of non-standard hours.

Cascade Trigger

Publish the shift-matching formula — how many combined employees justify one shared node — so other multi-employer clusters can replicate the math directly.

Faith + Civic Micro-Node Network

Purpose

Use existing, already-inspected community space — houses of worship, civic halls — that sits empty on weekdays, instead of waiting for new buildings.

Setup

  1. Run a short compatibility check against each site's existing occupancy permit, restroom access, and outdoor space.
  2. Skip a from-scratch licensing application wherever a site already qualifies under an existing occupancy class.
  3. Set up one regional back office and one shared insurance policy covering every node in the network.
  4. Start with the two or three most compliance-ready sites, not the full network, to prove the shared model first.

Safety Protocol

Because these are small, distributed nodes without their own licensing department, oversight is centralized at the network level — one shared inspection calendar and one shared incident-reporting line, so no single small node is left to self-police.

Cascade Trigger

Once the shared back-office model supports five to ten small nodes safely, publish it as a turnkey network-in-a-box for other regions' congregations and civic groups.

Veteran and Retiree Caregiver Fast-Track

Purpose

Address the caregiver shortage directly by fast-tracking two specific, willing, and often-overlooked labor pools, rather than waiting on the general workforce pipeline.

Setup

  1. Partner directly with VA transition programs and retiree/AARP-style networks for recruitment.
  2. Build a credit-mapping table translating military or civilian caregiving experience into credential hours.
  3. Run background checks and pediatric first-aid certification in full for every fast-tracked candidate.
  4. Pair each new caregiver with a supervised practicum period before independent responsibility.

Safety Protocol

Standard background checks and pediatric first aid still apply in full — the fast-track shortens duplicate paperwork and redundant training, never the safety bar itself.

Cascade Trigger

Publish the credit-mapping table — which military or civilian experience counts toward which credential hours — so any state licensing board can adopt the same fast-track.

Rural Mobile Childcare Circuit

Purpose

Serve small, spread-out populations where no single town has enough demand on its own to support a fixed center.

Setup

  1. Map the towns to be served and set one published weekly route and schedule.
  2. Confirm one existing community building at each stop that already meets basic occupancy requirements.
  3. Equip the mobile unit or traveling team with the same safety materials required at a fixed site.
  4. Publish the schedule publicly so families and inspectors always know exactly where the unit is.

Safety Protocol

Vehicle and road-travel safety standards in addition to standard care ratios, plus a fixed, published schedule so families and inspectors always know exactly where the unit is on any given day.

Cascade Trigger

Once one circuit proves it can serve a multi-town rural area safely, publish the route-planning and staffing model so it can be redrawn for a different rural region's map.

School-Age Wraparound Extension

Purpose

Close the specific before/after-school and school-break gap for children who already have a daytime school placement but no coverage outside school hours.

Setup

  1. Confirm the school building's existing licensing and security already cover the wraparound hours requested.
  2. Recruit staffing from school paraprofessionals first, or a single partnering outside provider.
  3. Build a clear handoff log between the school day and the wraparound program, tied to the school's own attendance system.
  4. Pilot in one school before extending district-wide.

Safety Protocol

A clear handoff procedure between school staff and wraparound staff, with sign-out matched to the school's own attendance system, so no child is ever unaccounted-for between the two.

Cascade Trigger

Once one school district runs this cleanly, publish the staffing and handoff template for other districts with a similar school-day schedule.

Mixed-Use Housing Childcare Default

Purpose

Make childcare space the default in new mixed-use housing approvals, rather than something a developer adds only if a jurisdiction happens to ask for it.

Setup

  1. Add a childcare-ready ground-floor unit specification to the planning department's standard mixed-use checklist.
  2. Require independent street-level entrance and egress for that unit at the blueprint stage, not as a later retrofit.
  3. Review the childcare unit's fire and occupancy plan alongside the building's general plan, not separately.
  4. Apply the default to the next new mixed-use application rather than waiting for a future code cycle.

Safety Protocol

Ground-floor placement with its own street-level entrance and exit, separate from residential building access, reviewed at the same time as the building's general fire and occupancy plan.

Cascade Trigger

Once one jurisdiction adopts the default checklist, publish it so other planning departments can add the same line item without drafting new code language from scratch.

Childcare Liability Pool Demonstration

Purpose

Address the specific barrier of expensive or unavailable liability insurance that keeps small providers from opening or expanding at all.

Setup

  1. Recruit an initial cohort of small providers willing to pool risk under one policy.
  2. Set uniform inspection and incident-reporting standards as a condition of pool membership.
  3. Structure the pool's governance and claims process before accepting the first cohort, not after.
  4. Track claims data from day one to build the actuarial record needed to prove sustainability.

Safety Protocol

Pool membership requires the same inspection and incident-reporting standards as any licensed provider — the pool changes who bears the cost of insurance, never the safety bar for joining it.

Cascade Trigger

Once the pool shows a sustainable claims record over one full year, publish its actuarial and governance structure so other states can stand up their own pool instead of negotiating one from zero.

Provider Shared Back-Office Cooperative

Purpose

Free small, independent providers from spending their limited time on payroll, compliance paperwork, and grant applications instead of care.

Setup

  1. Recruit a founding cohort of independent providers willing to share one payroll and compliance system.
  2. Set a proportional cost-sharing formula tied to each provider's size.
  3. Keep each provider's individual licensing relationship and inspection record fully intact and separate.
  4. Start with payroll and compliance-calendar sharing before adding grant-writing support.

Safety Protocol

Consolidating paperwork must not create a single point of failure — each member provider keeps its own direct licensing relationship and inspection record even while back-office work is centrally supported.

Cascade Trigger

Publish the cooperative's cost-sharing formula and service menu so providers in a different region can form their own cooperative rather than copying the exact organization.

AI Paperwork Reduction and Slot Matching Pilot

Purpose

Cut the administrative time providers and families spend on subsidy applications and slot-matching — a real barrier to using capacity that already exists.

Setup

  1. Scope the tool narrowly: pre-filling known eligibility fields and matching open slots to waitlisted families.
  2. Route every suggested eligibility or placement decision through a human caseworker for approval.
  3. Keep the tool's data under the same privacy rules as the paper process it replaces.

Glossary of Terms

Cascadability

How easily a single working example — one city's zoning fix, one state's waiver — can be copied elsewhere with minimal rework. A highly cascadable move ships as a reusable template (a form, a rule, a checklist) that a different jurisdiction can adopt in hours, not months.

Friction Cost

The time, paperwork, and uncertainty a family or provider absorbs before ever reaching the actual price of care — a duplicate inspection, an ambiguous approval, a form asking for the same information three different ways. Distinct from the cost of care itself, and often the larger barrier.

Slot Creation

Adding a childcare space that didn't exist before — converting a room, licensing a new home, opening a mobile stop — as distinct from a subsidy that helps a family afford a slot someone else already had.

Demand Subsidy

Funding that helps a family pay for care that already exists — a voucher, a tax credit, a sliding fee scale. Necessary, but does nothing on its own to create new capacity; this file consistently pairs it with supply-side action rather than treating it as a complete fix by itself.

Supply Subsidy

Funding directed at creating capacity itself — a conversion grant, a startup loan, a facilities fund — rather than at helping a family afford a slot that already exists. The counterpart to Demand Subsidy.

Liability Pooling

Spreading insurance risk across a group of small providers so no single one is priced out individually, the way a municipal or small-business risk pool already works. Changes who bears the cost of coverage, never the underlying safety standard required to join.

Micro-Node

A small, individual care site — a single room in a congregation, a corner of a community center — meant to be one of many in a distributed network rather than a single large center. Depends on shared back-office support (see Shared Services Alliance), since no individual node has the scale to run its own.

Shared Services Alliance

A group of independent providers sharing one payroll system, one compliance calendar, and one purchasing arrangement, while each keeps their own direct licensing relationship with the regulator. Lets small operators reach the efficiency a large chain gets from scale.

Family Child Care Home

A licensed provider operating out of their own home, typically serving a small group of children — a distinct licensing category from a center, usually with lighter facility requirements and a lower staff-ratio ceiling.

Mixed-Delivery System

An approach that deliberately supports several care models at once — centers, family child care homes, faith-based nodes, employer sites — rather than steering every family toward one preferred type. Underpins this file's Family Choice framing throughout.

Default Approval

A permitting approach where a qualifying application is approved automatically unless a specific, timely objection is raised — reversing the usual default of requiring affirmative approval before anything proceeds. Used in this file's zoning and licensing fast-track proposals.

Federalism Bridge

A federal action designed specifically to be adopted by states with minimal rewriting — a model template, a waiver, a clarified rule — rather than a federal program that centralizes delivery itself. Keeps administration with states while removing the friction of each one drafting its own version from scratch.

Procurement Preference

Adding childcare investment as one scoring factor — among several, never a pass/fail requirement — when government evaluates bids for a contract. Uses existing purchasing power to reward behavior already considered desirable, without excluding smaller bidders who don't yet offer it.

Workforce Ladder

A pay structure that ties raises to specific, verifiable milestones — credential level, tenure, added responsibility — so caregivers have a visible path to higher pay without leaving the field. Aimed directly at turnover, since flat pay with no advancement is the most commonly cited reason caregivers leave.

Care Desert

An area where licensed childcare supply falls well short of the children who need it. The commonly used threshold, established by the Center for American Progress, is more than three children under five for every licensed slot — the starting condition this file's supply-side proposals are meant to address.

Parent Workday Saved

A dashboard metric used throughout this file: a workday a parent was able to keep because a childcare gap that would have forced them to miss it was filled. Used instead of a raw slot count because it ties new capacity directly to the economic case for building it.

Safety Ratio

The number of children one caregiver may be responsible for at once, set by licensing rules and varying by age group. Treated throughout this file as a floor that no speed or cost-cutting proposal is allowed to lower.

Inspection Cadence

How often a licensed site is actually inspected, as distinct from whether an inspection requirement exists on paper. This file's Child Safety Audit concept specifically targets the gap between the two — a rule that's rarely checked provides little of the protection a rule that's regularly checked does.

Privacy Minimum

Collecting only the family or child data a specific safety or eligibility purpose actually requires, and no more. This file's standard for any proposed data system, tracking mechanism, or dashboard.

Resilience Center

A pre-designated care site equipped with backup power and stocked supplies, activated during a declared emergency to keep care available for the responders, medical staff, and utility crews who need to be at work when ordinary centers close.

Wraparound Care

Care that fills the hours around a child's existing placement — before and after the school day, or during school breaks — rather than covering a full day on its own. Named for “wrapping around” a schedule the child already has, such as school.

Intergenerational Care

A model serving both children and older adults at a shared site, useful where demand for either group alone wouldn't justify a dedicated facility. Requires reconciling child-care and elder-care licensing rules typically written assuming separate buildings.

Shift-Matched Care

Care hours deliberately sized to a specific shift pattern — nights, rotating, weekends — rather than a standard daytime schedule, so workers whose hours fall outside the typical school day still have a real option.

Scoreboard Governance

The rules for who may publish, edit, and audit a public metrics dashboard — ideally including a party independent of the agency being measured — so the numbers stay credible as scrutiny increases over time.
